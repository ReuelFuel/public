Magnesium, ATP, and why

Yohance  ·  18 Jun 2026  ·  6 min read

Magnesium is the fourth most abundant mineral in the body and a cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions. Most critically, it is required for ATP — adenosine triphosphate — to be biologically active. Every time a cell uses energy, it uses magnesium.

So when roughly 80% of adults are estimated to be deficient in it, that's not a minor nutritional gap. It's a systemic problem at the level of cellular energy production.

What magnesium actually does

The ATP molecule that powers cellular work doesn't circulate as ATP alone — it circulates as a magnesium-ATP complex (Mg-ATP). Without sufficient magnesium, the ATP produced by the mitochondria can't be properly utilised. The cell has energy in theory, but can't access it in practice.

"Magnesium is involved in more biochemical reactions than any other mineral."

Dr. Carolyn Dean, The Magnesium Miracle

Beyond ATP, magnesium is involved in DNA synthesis, protein synthesis, muscle contraction, nerve signal transmission, and blood glucose regulation. Its deficiency doesn't announce itself clearly — it shows up as fatigue, muscle cramps, poor sleep, anxiety, and over time, insulin resistance.

Why deficiency is so common

Two forces converge here. First, modern agricultural soil is significantly depleted in magnesium compared to 50 years ago — intensive farming and fertiliser use have stripped minerals without replacing them. The vegetables that exist today contain less magnesium than the same vegetables grown in 1950.

Second, many of the things modern people consume in excess — refined sugar, alcohol, caffeine — actively deplete magnesium by increasing urinary excretion. The more processed the diet, the faster the depletion.

Key point

Magnesium deficiency rarely shows up on a standard blood test — serum magnesium represents less than 1% of total body stores. You can be severely deficient with a "normal" result.

What to do about it

Food sources high in magnesium include dark leafy greens (especially spinach), pumpkin seeds, dark chocolate, legumes, and whole grains. The challenge is that modern versions of these foods contain less than they used to — so dietary sources alone may not be sufficient.

Supplementation is worth considering, particularly magnesium glycinate (well absorbed, gentle on the stomach) or magnesium malate (supports energy production specifically). Start low, assess response, and treat the body as the complex system it is.

This is one piece of a larger picture. Mineral depletion in the food supply is one of the quietest and most consequential changes to human nutrition in the last century. Magnesium is just the most striking example.
